## The problem

On {clients.books}, each merchant syncs a subset of a shared catalogue to their
own storefront. Working out what belongs in that subset meant walking the
category tree and joining categories to their products and those products to the
per-store product table — three tables, the largest of them in the tens of
millions of rows.

Categories nest, so the traversal was recursive: expand a category to its
descendants, then collect the products under all of them. Recursive traversal
joined to a table that size is expensive per run, and this ran per store, on a
schedule.

The symptom was worst-case duration, not average. Most stores were fine. The
large ones took long enough that syncs overlapped their own schedule, and once
that happens the backlog is self-sustaining.

I spent real time on the query itself first — restructuring the recursion,
comparing plans, getting indexes onto the columns the planner actually wanted.
It got better. It did not get better enough, and the reason was structural: the
query was recomputing a category-to-product relationship that changes far less
often than it is read.

## What I did

Built a derived table keyed the way the question is asked — category to product,
already flattened — and pointed the sync at it.

The recursion happens when the relationship changes, not every time a store
syncs. At read time the sync does an indexed lookup against one table.

**Worst-case query time fell by more than an order of magnitude.**

The trade is explicit and worth naming: I took on a consistency problem to get
rid of a latency problem. A derived table is a cache with a schema, and it is
wrong the moment its sources move and it doesn't.

## What I decided not to do

I stopped tuning the join. Past a point, more index work on a recursive query
over three growing tables is effort spent making the wrong shape faster.

I didn't reach for a materialised view or a caching layer. The database doesn't
give me an incrementally maintained view here, and a cache in front of the query
would have kept the expensive query on the cold path and added an eviction
policy to reason about. The table is the cache, and it's one I control.

Index migrations and splitting the hourly job into per-organisation runs both
happened around the same time, and both helped — a slow tenant no longer delayed
the rest. Neither is why the number moved, and I'd rather say that plainly than
present four changes as equal contributors to one result.

## How it shipped

The interesting work wasn't building the table, it was keeping it honest. I
wrote the sync that maintains it from its sources, then went looking for rows
where the derived table and the source tables disagreed — because the failure
mode of this design isn't slowness, it's silently serving a stale answer that
looks fine.

Reconciling those mismatches was where the design got tested. Every one of them
was a path that mutated a source without telling the derived table, and each one
had to be found rather than reasoned about.

If I were handing this over, that's the part I'd point at. The table is easy.
Knowing what invalidates it is the whole job.
