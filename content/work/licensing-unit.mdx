## The problem

This was {clients.cards}. The client's requirements document named the fault in
its opening paragraph, and it was blunt about the cost:

> Currently, within a client account, a user and a microsite are treated as the
> same entity. This causes confusion, especially when a client wants to reassign
> a microsite to a new person. [...] the custom URL (which often includes the
> original user's name) and the associated analytics remain unchanged, leading
> to inaccurate reporting and data inconsistencies.

One record stood for two different things: a person who signs in, and a
published card that a company pays for. Editing the card's name and email
changed what a visitor saw, but the record underneath still belonged to the
original person. The public URL kept their name. The reporting kept crediting
them.

That single conflation had been arriving as three separate complaints —
reassignment doesn't work, the URLs are wrong, the analytics are wrong. They
were not three problems. They were one modelling mistake seen from three
angles.

It was a billing problem too. Plan quantity had always been counted in people,
so a company paid per seat. Once one person could hold more than one card, the
licence had to move with the card.

## What I did

I separated the two ideas and moved the licensed unit onto the card.

The billing columns across the subscription, plan, cart, order, coupon and
discount tables were renamed from a headcount to a card count, and the gates
that had blocked adding people were removed. People became unlimited. Cards
became the thing you buy.

Reassignment stopped being an edit. Handing a card to someone else archives the
existing one, creates a new one for the new owner, and carries the site content
and its edit history across. The endpoint refuses the cases that would quietly
corrupt something: a card that is already archived, a transfer to the person who
already owns it, and an invitee who declined, cancelled, or never answered.

Published URLs had to survive all of it. A proposed slug is checked against
company slugs, retired company slugs, retired personal slugs, and the other
cards in the same company before it is accepted. Changing a slug records the old
one as a redirect automatically, and any redirect already pointing at the new
slug is removed in the same step — so a URL never resolves through a hop back to
itself.

## What I decided not to do

The word "user" appeared in far more places than the licence did. Renaming all
of them would have been consistent, and wrong.

The analytics counters count people, not licences. They kept their names,
because a company genuinely does want to know how many people it has, and
renaming them would have quietly changed what a number on a dashboard meant.

Two flags used "user" to mean something else entirely — who is allowed to edit a
template, and whether an order came from a customer or from staff. Same word,
unrelated concept. Left alone.

The administrative surface was specified in full and deferred to a second phase.
It is internal, it is not on the customer path, and folding it into the same
change would have doubled the area under test for no customer-visible benefit.

One API field kept its old name. Renaming it is a hard cutover that breaks the
frontend the moment it lands, so it waits and ships in lockstep with the
frontend rather than ahead of it.

## How it shipped

The renames preserve data, so there was no backfill to write — but they had to
run before the code deployed, because the code refers to the new column names
and would crash against the old schema.

One coordination step sat outside the codebase entirely. A property had to be
created in the CRM before deploy; without it the sync does not fail, it silently
drops the field, which is worse than failing.

Verification was manual and written before it was needed: cap a company's
licences, add more people than the cap and confirm none are blocked, create
cards up to the cap, confirm the next is refused, release one and confirm it is
accepted, then reduce the subscription quantity and watch what happens.
Rollback was the migrations in reverse.

The work is built and reviewed. It has not been released.

That last verification step is the one I would point at. When a subscription's
quantity drops below the number of active cards, the excess are not deleted and
not archived. They are converted back to the free digital card that every person
already gets. The company loses the licence, not the content — a downgrade
expressed as a change of type rather than a deletion.
